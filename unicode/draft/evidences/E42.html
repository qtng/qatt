Nguyễn Quang Hồng, "Quốc âm tân tự – một phương án chữ Việt ghi âm thế kỷ 19", Tạp chí Hán Nôm, no. 1, 1986. Read in the online archive of the journal, https://hannom.vass.gov.vn/web/tchn/data/8602v.htm (the page is headed 1/1989, but it holds issue 1 of 1986: it also contains the article of Mai Hồng and Nguyễn Hữu Mùi on printing that Trần Nghĩa cites as issue 1 of 1986, and Trần Nghĩa, Tạp chí Hán Nôm, no. 2, 1987, https://hannom.vass.gov.vn/web/tchn/data/8702v.htm, cites this article as issue 1 of 1986). The web version gives no page numbers and does not show the two photographs the article refers to. Only short passages are quoted here; the article is under copyright.

Findings

  • Source: a thin volume with the shelfmark AB.636 (sic; this proposal has AB.630, see Open), 15 × 28 cm, 8 leaves (16 pages): two manuscripts of the same work bound together, 4 leaves each, with the same number of characters. The first seems to be a copy of the second, which looks older; whether the second is the author's own is not known. Both have copying errors, so they can be read against each other. Stamps show that the volume was in the library of the École française d'Extrême-Orient more than half a century before 1986. (Proposal 3.1.)
  • Dating and author: the taboo form of 華 (after Ngô Đức Thọ: Hồ Thị Hoa, mother of Thiệu Trị) places the work under the Nguyễn, in the middle to late 19th century. The signature 五星聚斗南城居士阮子書 names a lay scholar surnamed Nguyễn with the pen name Ngũ Tinh Tụ Đẩu, living in the city of Nam Định; his real name is not known. According to the preface he made the script for his own use. (E30, E31; proposal 2.1.)
  • Design: 132 simple signs, 22 Cán (cán tự) and 110 Chi (chi tự), each of four strokes from the four basic strokes horizontal, vertical, dot and slant. A Chi is made by moving the first stroke of its Cán to one of five other positions, two on the left and three on the right. In the text, 3 of the 110 signs have only three strokes, probably by omission; one Chi of KHONG is missing from the table, so it shows 109 (E29). No influence of kana or of the Chinese phonetic alphabet is seen. (Proposal 4.1.)
  • Cán: read by their Nôm annotations as Ông, Ngông, Hông, Gông, Công, Lông, Trông, Đông, Nông, Tông, Thông, Nhông, Chông, Giông, Xông, Không, Sông, Rông, Mông, Bông, Vông, Phông: the initials of Vietnamese, the zero initial included. D- and GI- fall together in Giông, while TR-, S- and R- are kept apart. (Proposal 7.1 follows this and names the 14th GIONG.)
  • Chi: read as rhymes, with the medial -u-/-o- included, for example 1a to 1e Oai, Ôn, Oan, Uy, Âm and 2a to 2e Nghe, Ngon, Ngan, Nghi, Ngâm. The author warns that the 110 Chi cannot be matched one to one with the rhymes, that several annotations share a rhyme, and that 43 annotations carry the 口 radical as a hint to a deviant reading. (Proposal 4.1.)
  • Tones: eight, âm with a half circle and dương with a full circle; left foot bình, left shoulder thượng, right shoulder khứ, right foot nhập (E14; proposal 4.4).
  • Combination: hợp thư for a Cán and a Chi written together, for example Đoan, the Cán Đông on the left and the Chi 1c Oan on the right, with the half circle at the lower left (âm bình). The reverse order, Chi left and Cán right, seems to be accepted too, for example Ông from the Chi 1a and the Cán 1. Spelling: Đông + Oan → Đoan, Ngông + Hoa → Ngoa, Ông + Nghi → Y. (Proposal 4.2, 4.3: the broad reading of Hợp Thư.)
  • Tham thư: a Chi written above a pair, for syllables of complex articulation. Example: the Chi 1d Uy with the Cán 1 Ông, and the Chi 16c Khoen above; the source describes its sound as "spoken quickly, the tongue curled ..., the lips drawn in", read tentatively as oen or oeng (E40; proposal 4.3).
  • Counts: 176 with Cán alone, 880 with Chi alone, 880 Chi + Cán (46 of them entering tone), 18180 Cán + Chi (1932 entering tone) as printed; the author finds the counts partly unexplained (E36, where the source reads 18,480).
  • Assessment: a phonetic script built from the strokes of Chinese characters, a counterpart to kana and the Chinese phonetic alphabet in the history of Vietnamese writing; it had no echo, its signs are hard to tell apart (equal numbers of strokes), and the author gave no specimen text. A valuable source for the history of Vietnamese writing and phonology.
