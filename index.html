The script

Quốc Âm Tân Tự (國音新字, “new script for the sounds of the national language”, abbreviated QATT) is a semisyllabic writing system. It is known from a single text, preserved in two manuscripts at the Institute of Hán Nôm Studies in Hanoi (AB.630) and dated to the reign of Emperor Thiệu Trị (1841–1847).

The system consists of 22 base symbols, the Cán, each drawn with three strokes. A base exists in seven forms: one without a marking stroke and six with a short marking stroke at one of the six stroke ends. Two components form a character: the left part gives the onset, the right part the rhyme, so the sample above is read xa. There are 110 rhyme parts for writing Vietnamese, and eight tones are marked with circles and semicircles in the corners of a character.

Unicode proposal

QATT is not yet part of Unicode. The QATT Unicode Proposal – Draft encodes the script as 164 characters in the Supplementary Multilingual Plane: 154 components, 2 joiners and 8 tone marks, in a block of 176 code points. Components are encoded in reading order, and every combination is written with an explicit joiner.

The block position is not assigned yet. The page QATT Unicode shows the draft block as a code chart and as a list of all code points with their names, properties and the tone marks.

Decodings

The source explains the script with a table of rhymes written in Chữ Nôm. Many of its annotation characters have ambiguous readings, or are not in any dictionary, so the script cannot be decoded without additional hypotheses. The page Decodings shows the rhyme table of the symbols in the different decodings, the one used on this site and the one of Omniglot, and lists the hypotheses behind them. You can choose a decoding, change the size of the symbols and pivot the table.
